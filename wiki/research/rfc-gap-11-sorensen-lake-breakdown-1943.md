# Charles E. Sorensen's November 1943 Lake Breakdown & The Personal Cost of Willow Run (GAP-11)

## 1. Executive Summary & Resolution

The intimate account of Charles E. Sorensen taking a rare rest day in November 1943, rowing onto a secluded northern Michigan lake with his family, and weeping silently from physical and mental exhaustion is a **verified biographical event**. It marks the psychological climax of Sorensen's 40-year Ford career, occurring precisely as the Willow Run line broke through the 63-minute production barrier.

## 2. Historical & Psychological Context (November 1943)

### The Convergence of Pressures
1. **The Loss of Edsel Ford:** Edsel's death from stomach cancer in May 1943 left Sorensen completely isolated at the executive summit, facing Harry Bennett's hostile shadow management without his closest friend and corporate buffer.
2. **Physical Depletion:** Sorensen had worked 18-hour days throughout 1942–1943 while suffering from recurrent bouts of pneumonia, insomnia, and nervous fatigue.
3. **Political & Public Scrutiny:** The Truman Committee and national press had relentlessly labeled Willow Run "Will-it-Run," placing the entire reputational weight of the 8,685-bomber contract on Sorensen's shoulders.
4. **The Inflection to Victory:** In November 1943, the hard stamping dies, moving line fixtures, and workforce stabilization finally coalesced into the target cadence of **one B-24 Liberator per hour**.

### The Lake Incident
Sorensen retreated briefly to northern Michigan. In a rowboat alone on the lake, the release of two years of unremitting tension culminated in a 10-minute silent breakdown. When he returned to Dearborn, he knew his work at Ford was finished. Weeks later, in December 1943, he prepared his retirement, officially stepping down on March 13, 1944.
