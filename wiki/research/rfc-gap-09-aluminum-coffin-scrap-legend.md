# Debunking the 'South American Aluminum Coffin' Scrapping Legend (GAP-09)

## 1. Executive Summary & Resolution

The popular claim that Ford buyers procured thousands of aluminum coffins destined for South America to melt down into B-24 Liberator skins is an **apocryphal shop-floor myth**. While non-ferrous scrap was aggressively collected for plant maintenance, foundry molds, and non-structural tooling fixtures, metallurgical constraints prohibited remelted scrap from being used in primary airframe skins.

## 2. Archival & Metallurgical Grounding

### War Production Board Limitation Order L-64
In March 1942, the WPB issued **Limitation Order L-64**, prohibiting the manufacture of civilian burial caskets utilizing aluminum, bronze, copper, or zinc. Mortuary suppliers were forced to liquidate existing stocks and convert entirely to wood or masonite. News coverage of casket metal bans and national aluminum scrap drives created the cultural basis for the myth.

### Metallurgy Constraints: Alclad 24S-T vs. Secondary Ingot
* **Tensile & Yield Requirements:** The B-24's Davis wing and fuselage panels required certified **Alclad 24S-T** (copper-magnesium-aluminum alloy with pure aluminum protective cladding) capable of withstanding 62,000 psi tensile stress.
* **Secondary Scrap Contamination:** Remelting consumer or funerary goods yields heterogeneous secondary aluminum with uncontrolled silicon/iron impurities, making it chemically and structurally ineligible for USAAF flight certification.
* **Primary Supply Pipeline:** All primary skin coils and sheet stock were supplied directly from Alcoa and Reynolds Metals under WPB Controlled Materials Plan (CMP) priority allocations.

## 3. Script & Fact-Check Guidance

* **Classification:** Apocryphal legend / Folklore.
* **Editorial Action:** Debunk the story on-screen to highlight the extreme metallurgical rigor and chemical purity demanded by USAAF aircraft manufacturing standards.
