# RFC GAP-17: Multi-Vocal Attribution of the Moving Assembly Line

> **Executive Summary:** Executive memoirs (*My Forty Years with Ford*, 1956; *My Life and Work*, 1922) consistently frame the moving assembly line as an anonymous executive milestone or general slaughterhouse inspiration. Primary shop-floor documentation—centered on William "Pa" Klann's 1955 oral history (BFRC Accession 499) and Clarence W. Avery's time-study ledgers (Accession 433)—establishes a precise, contested division of labor: Klann proposed the concept from Swift & Co. observations and executed the April 1913 flywheel magneto trial; Avery engineered the conveyor speeds and progressive work stations; Peter E. Martin managed plant retooling; and Charles Sorensen provided executive shielding and raw pacing enforcement.

## 2. Archival Reconstruction: The April 1913 Flywheel Magneto Breakthrough

1. **The Chicago Inception (Fall 1912):** William Klann toured Swift & Company's Chicago packing plant, observing overhead gravity trolleys where beef carcasses were systematically disassembled across fixed butcher stations.
2. **The Magneto Test Bench (April 1913):** Klann mounted 29 flywheel magneto assembly operations along a waist-high metal slide rail pulled by a manual windlass:
   - **Stationary bench assembly time:** 20 minutes per unit.
   - **Initial moving line time:** 13 minutes, 10 seconds.
   - **Calibrated line speed (Avery adjustments, summer 1913):** 5 minutes per unit (300% efficiency gain).
3. **Chassis Progressive Assembly (January 1914):** Avery and Sorensen expanded the moving principle to the full chassis on Highland Park's main floor, cutting total vehicle assembly time from **12 hours, 8 minutes** to **93 minutes**.

## 3. Historiographical Resolution

Executive memoirs deliberately flattened specialized floor-level engineering to cultivate the corporate myth of founder genius. The moving assembly line was not an instantaneous flash of inspiration by Henry Ford or Charles Sorensen, but an iterative synthesis conceived by **William Klann** (process concept & prototype), formalized by **Clarence Avery** (speed & motion calculus), authorized by **Peter Martin** (plant floor allocation), and enforced by **Charles Sorensen** (executive scale).
